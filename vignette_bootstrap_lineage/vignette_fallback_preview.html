A Vignette Concerning Its Own Citability

A companion document to the DeScideratum resampling lineage

What this document is, said plainly, before it gets cute about it

This is a vignette — a short, self-contained illustrative document, a term borrowed deliberately from the R and Bioconductor statistical computing world, where it has meant exactly this for decades: real methods, original prose, a synthetic teaching example, clearly labeled as a companion piece rather than the source material itself. Nothing in this document reproduces the text of any paper it discusses. Everything in this document is original, written now, for the purpose of testing something.

The something being tested is narrow and specific: can a citation be written two ways in the same document, about the same real statistical methods, and can a reader tell the difference just by encountering them? One way is old — as old as scholarship itself. The other way is the actual subject of the project this vignette belongs to.

Interlude, in which the document notices what it is

It is worth saying, since the alternative is pretending otherwise: this paragraph exists to test whether prose can be self-aware without being annoying about it. A paper does not normally know it is a paper. This one, for a few sentences, does. It was written by an AI assistant working with an independent researcher, on a Tuesday, for the explicit purpose of finding out whether a citation could be made to run rather than merely sit on a page. It has no data of its own — the numbers below are illustrative, invented for the occasion, not drawn from any study of anything.

Part One: the classic citation, doing what citations have always done

Suppose you have a small sample of measurements and you want to know how much you should trust their average. The oldest rigorous answer to that question that this vignette will discuss is the bootstrap (Efron, 1979) — resample your own data with replacement, over and over, and let the spread of those resamples stand in for the uncertainty you can't observe directly. Two refinements followed: a bias-and-skew correction that sharpens the resulting interval (Efron & Tibshirani, 1993), and a further calibration step that resamples the correction itself to test how stable it really is (Davison & Hinkley, 1997).

Read that paragraph again. It is a completely ordinary piece of scholarly writing. Three names, three years, three parenthetical pointers, sitting inertly on the page, asking you to trust that the author read and correctly understood three real papers.

Closing note, addressed to whoever is reading this


If Part One and Part Two felt like different kinds of writing, that difference is the whole argument this vignette was built to carry. Neither replaces the other everywhere. But for the specific, narrow category of citation where a paper says "I ran this earlier method" — this is what that citation could actually look like, instead of merely claiming to.
